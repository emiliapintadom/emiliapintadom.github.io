Gender and Politics in the Arab World

Gender as a Mechanism of Power: Women in Palestine

Emilia Pintado Mariscal May 2026 9 min read

In March 1994, Yasser Arafat returned from exile in Tunis to establish the Palestinian Authority. He returned to a society whose political infrastructure had been built and sustained, in large part, by women. During the First Intifada of 1987-1993, Palestinian women had organised underground schools, built healthcare and food networks, and coordinated collective action across the Occupied Territories. When tens of thousands of men were arrested, deported, or killed, women filled the leadership vacuum. The Palestinian Authority that Arafat built, however, included almost none of these women. This exclusion was neither accidental nor exceptional. It was the latest instance of a pattern that has defined Palestinian politics since the 1920s: women are mobilised when the national movement needs them, and pushed aside once it produces results.

This essay argues that gender in Palestinian politics has not simply reflected inequality, but has actively produced it. Rather than being a side issue, gender has been central to how political power is organised and reproduced across different historical periods and ideological contexts. To understand women’s political position in Palestine, it is necessary to ask not only who was excluded, but how that exclusion actually worked. Drawing on Islah Jad’s Palestinian Women’s Activism (2018) and Joan Scott’s framework of gender as power, the essay traces this pattern across the British Mandate, PLO, and intifada periods, before turning to the international response to Oslo and the reconfiguration of women’s activism that followed.

Joan Scott’s argument that gender is “a primary way of signifying relationships of power” (Scott, 1986, p. 1067) is a useful starting point because it shifts attention away from gender as a descriptive category and toward gender as something that does political work. Those who hold power use gender to define who belongs in political life and who does not, and crucially, they do so in ways that make those boundaries look natural rather than constructed.

During the British Mandate period of the 1920s-1948, Palestinian women’s organised activism was led by urban, educated women from elite families. Their activism presented Palestinian women as modern and educated to a colonial audience that judged societies partly through the status of their women. The priority was national legitimacy, not women’s rights as such (Jad, 2018, pp. 22-28). When the Great Revolt of 1936-39 erupted, elite women were instructed to veil in public as a gesture of solidarity with the villages. Jad (2018, pp. 22-28) reads this not as tradition reasserting itself but as class antagonism expressed through gender: the peasantry used the bodies of elite women to push back against the cultural authority of the urban leadership. The same mechanism that had enabled women’s mobilisation was being used to limit it, in the service of a different political interest.

The PLO era of the 1960s-1980s appears more progressive, but the same logic operates. As Leila Ahmed (1992, pp. 144-168) has argued, there is often a gap between the rhetoric of liberation and the actual practice of sharing power. Within the PLO, particularly under Fateh, the position was fairly explicit: national liberation first, women’s rights afterwards. Jad (2018, pp. 38-45) shows that no serious gender platform was ever developed and that women’s committees remained subordinate to the male leadership. The revolutionary language brought women in, while the gender hierarchy ensured they stayed in a supporting role.

The First Intifada (1987-1993) is perhaps the most striking example of this pattern. Mass women’s committees organised tens of thousands of women across the West Bank and Gaza, building the schools, clinics, and supply networks that kept the uprising going (Jad, 2018, pp. 52-61). These were politically functional institutions with real accountability to their members. Yet when Oslo converted the intifada’s momentum into formal institutional structures, women were largely absent from what was built. The Palestinian Authority was formed by a leadership that had been in exile throughout the uprising, and the women’s committees were not brought in. As Scott (1986) would lead us to expect, gender was the mechanism through which the political gains of the intifada were distributed among those who had not produced them.

The international response to Oslo raises a question that follows naturally from what has been argued so far: did the foreign attention and funding that came with the peace process disrupt the pattern of gender exclusion or reinforce it? The evidence, as Jad (2004) presents it, points fairly clearly toward the latter. The proliferation of internationally funded women’s NGOs after 1993 did not fill the gap left by the sidelining of the mass women’s movement. It displaced that movement, turning what had been a politically rooted organisation into something quite different.

Membership in the mass women’s committees dropped by 37% after 1993, and new recruitment nearly stopped (Jad, 2018, p. 68). The women who had built relationships across villages and refugee camps were replaced by what Jad (2018, p. 72) calls the “femocrat”: a professionally trained, English-speaking feminist working in an office, skilled at writing grant reports but without meaningful connections to the constituencies she was supposed to represent. A mass committee is answerable to its members and focused on building lasting collective capacity. An NGO is answerable to its funders and focused on delivering a specific project within a specific timeframe. The women’s committees were social and political structures; the NGOs that replaced them were closer to service providers, and that substitution was not politically neutral (Jad, 2004).

The 1998 Palestinian Model Parliament illustrates this clearly. Examined through both Jad’s (2004) analysis and a retrospective by five of its organisers (Abu-Dayyeh et al., 2015), it shows what NGO-ization looked like in practice. The campaign had real scale: WCLAC trained activists across the West Bank and Gaza, collected 13,000 signatures, and broadcast a Model Parliament live on Palestinian television in March 1998 (Abu-Dayyeh et al., 2015, pp. 18-34). The demands were meaningful: restrictions on polygamy, better divorce rights, a minimum marriage age, and more equal inheritance laws. Despite all of this, the campaign produced almost no legal change. Hamas attacked it as a Western-funded plot to undermine shari’a, and the attacks worked. Women activists were blocked from poorer neighbourhoods, and the campaign eventually collapsed amid organisational distrust.

The organisers’ own retrospective is clear about why. Randa Siniora writes that the campaign lacked political anchoring, noting that “the NGO community cannot and should not take sole responsibility for advancing social change” (Abu-Dayyeh et al., 2015, p. 67). No political party defended the campaign when it came under pressure. Said’s (1978, pp. 3-5) concept of Orientalism helps explain the specific vulnerability this created. The campaign drew on the language of CEDAW and international human rights frameworks, which made it credible to Western donors but gave Hamas its most effective line of attack: the campaign could be framed as foreign interference precisely because its vocabulary had been shaped more by donor priorities than by local political terms. The features that made it fundable also made it easier to delegitimise.

Women’s political agency did not simply disappear after Oslo, and it would be an oversimplification to read what happened as a straightforward story of decline. The agency was reconfigured into different forms, including grassroots peacebuilding, cross-conflict cooperation, and community-level activism. The real question, however, is whether these new forms of participation were able to challenge the structural conditions that had produced women’s exclusion in the first place.

Lila Abu-Lughod (1998, pp. 7-15) argues that agency should be understood in its actual context rather than judged against an external standard of what meaningful political participation looks like. Women who could no longer organise through mass committees found other ways to participate, and the drop in committee membership is better read as evidence of constraint than of withdrawal. Kimberle Crenshaw (1991, pp. 1244-1245) adds that these constraints were not uniform. Class, education, and geography all shaped what forms of participation were available after Oslo, and treating “Palestinian women after Oslo” as a single category would reproduce the erasure that Jad (2018) argues against throughout her book.

The most visible new form was what Giulia Daniele (2014, pp. 82-94) describes as “everyday peacebuilding”: grassroots dialogue and cross-conflict cooperation outside formal negotiation processes, including initiatives like Women in Black and Jerusalem Link. The political commitment of those involved was genuine, but Daniele (2014, pp. 101-108) points to real limitations. Participation was concentrated among urban, educated women already connected to activist networks, while those from more marginalised backgrounds were largely absent. These initiatives also had no connection to the formal decision-making processes that determined political outcomes. They generated dialogue but not institutional power, reflecting the same structural limitation as NGO-ization: women were participating, but in spaces that could not challenge the distribution of power that had made those limited spaces the only ones available.

Badran and Cooke (1990, pp. 20-23) argue that Arab feminist thought develops from within local traditions rather than being transplanted from outside, and this helps clarify what was missing. The intifada-era mass committees were locally rooted, accountable to their members, and embedded in the communities they worked in. What replaced them after Oslo was oriented outward, toward donors, international frameworks, or Israeli counterparts, rather than inward toward Palestinian communities. The structural pattern visible from the 1920s onward was not disrupted by these new forms of activism. It continued to shape them.

This essay has argued that gender in Palestinian politics functions as a structural mechanism of power rather than simply reflecting a pre-existing social inequality. What runs through all of the cases examined here is the dynamic that Scott (1986) identified at the start: gender being used not just to reflect who is excluded from power, but to actively produce and justify that exclusion. What makes this pattern so persistent is not that Palestinian political culture is uniquely resistant to women’s participation, but that the forms through which political power has been organised, from the PLO factions to the PA to the NGO sector, have consistently reproduced the same structural logic regardless of their ideological differences. The 1998 Model Parliament captures this most sharply: a campaign with genuine reach and serious demands that collapsed not for lack of effort but because the organisational form it took could not withstand political pressure without a grassroots base behind it (Abu-Dayyeh et al., 2015). Jad’s (2018) work suggests that the intifada-era committees came closest to building that kind of base, precisely because they were locally rooted and internally accountable in ways that neither NGOs nor peacebuilding initiatives have managed to replicate. Whether that kind of organisation is possible under current conditions, given the fragmentation of Palestinian political life since Oslo, is something this essay cannot answer. However, the history examined here suggests that we are at least asking the right questions.
