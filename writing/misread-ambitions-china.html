Misread Ambitions: Reframing China’s Rise in the International System

Emilia Pintado Mariscal 2025 17 min read

Introduction

Over the past decade, China’s rising global influence has reshaped the international conversation on power, security, and order. China has expanded its naval capabilities, its global infrastructure networks, and engaged in diplomatic tensions with the United States. On this note, scholars and policymakers are questioning the intentions behind its foreign policy. Is China’s assertiveness under Xi Jinping a sign of expansionist ambition, or is it a defensive posture over sovereignty and preservation of the regime? Some experts, like Robert Blackwill and Ashley Tellis, argue that China’s long-term strategy seeks regional dominance through economic coercion and strategic pressure.[1] Others, such as David C. Kang and Alastair Iain Johnston, interpret China’s behavior as fundamentally defensive. They argue that China’s intentions are aimed at maintaining regional stability and managing external threats, not projecting power.[2] As Kang explains, “East Asian international relations remain remarkably stable not because of U.S. balancing or deterrence, but because states in the region have internalized a hierarchical order with China at its center.”[3] In other words, China’s growing role in the region does not mean it is trying to dominate the system. To support this argument, the research looks into China’s economic partnerships, the Belt and Road Initiative, China’s actions in the South China Sea, their domestic struggles, as well as its diplomatic posturing toward the United States. This paper argues that China’s assertive behavior comes from a place of insecurity and a desire to protect its regime, not from an attempt to change the current international order.

Expansionism

To take on this debate, it is relevant to first define and analyze what expansionism is. According to scholar Robert S. Ross expansionism refers to a state’s effort to extend its influence beyond its recognized borders, often using military power to achieve territorial, economical, or ideological goals.[4] It usually involves attempts to alter the status quo of the international system. Expansionist states tend to take actions that allow the expanding state to secure regional dominance or global reach. Expansionist behavior is marked by aggressive diplomacy, coercion, and a willingness to challenge rival powers.[5] Now, let us look into an example: the British Empire stands as one of the most well-documented and powerful examples of expansionism in modern history. Its rise was driven by a combination of economic ambition, naval supremacy, global territorial control, and a belief in the civilizing mission of the empire.[6] Analyzing the British Empire’s actions is helpful to fully comprehend the way expansionist states act. In the context of this paper, it will be used as a side to side comparison of foreign policies, comparing modern China and the 19th Century British Empire.

Britain’s expansionist behavior was visible in its control of territories across Asia, Africa, the Americas, and the Pacific. Britain actively seized land, intervened directly in local governance, imposed economic structures, and extracted resources. Britain also built a vast network of overseas bases, enabling it to sustain long-distance military operations and secure its colonies. British leaders promoted a sense of moral superiority, stating imperial rule was their duty. Expansion was not just strategic, it was ideologically driven and backed by a sense of entitlement to global leadership.[7]

If China were acting as an expansionist power, its behavior would look dramatically different from what we see today. First, China would likely maintain a network of overseas military bases stretching across the Indian Ocean, Africa, Latin America, and even the Mediterranean, similar to those of the British Empire or the modern United States.  Second, China would not limit its territorial ambitions to Taiwan and the South China Sea, it would likely press new claims in neighboring states like India and Indonesia. Third, China would be actively exporting ideology, using economic tools like the Belt and Road Initiative for political leverage, pressuring states to adopt Chinese models of governance.

Unlike historical expansionist powers such as the British Empire, China’s global military presence remains minimal and constrained in the region. As of now, China has only one official overseas military base, located in Djibouti. This base primarily supports anti-piracy operations, it is by no means a form of territorial control.[8] China has no permanently deployed military forces outside Asia, and has shown no interest to engage in foreign military interventions.[9] Instead of using its navy to enforce regime change or occupy territory, China’s maritime activity has focused on securing sea lanes of communication vital for its market trade.[10] In sum, the limited presence of China’s military and distinctiveness from other expansionist powers throughout history, suggest that China’s grand strategy remains fundamentally defensive.

Power transition theory

Beneath the surface of China’s rise, some analysts see more than just caution. They argue that Beijing is testing the limits of the international system to reshape it to its advantage. This interpretation is often supported by power transition theory, originally proposed by political scientist  A.F.K. Organski. His theory suggests that rising powers challenge the dominant order as they gain strength.[11] John Mearsheimer, a leading proponent of this view, argues that China’s growing military capability, assertive diplomacy, and regional posturing are part of a long-term strategy to displace U.S. influence and establish Chinese regional hegemony.[12] From this angle, China’s rise is not a reaction to external threats, but a proactive effort to modify the international system.

Power transition theory assumes that all rising powers seek to change the system once they become powerful enough, but China’s case does not fit that model. Unlike past revisionist powers like Nazi Germany or Imperial Japan, China has not built an alliance network, not exported ideology, and not engaged in major wars of conquest. Instead, it has sought to secure strategic access to energy and trade routes, hedge against U.S. pressure, and prevent isolation. These are the moves of a status quo actor with defensive priorities, not an expansionist one seeking to dominate.

As Alastair Iain Johnston has shown, China’s foreign policy behavior is generally cautious and risk-averse, noting that “China has not acted in ways typical of aggressive challengers to the international system.”[13]Instead of seeking to impose a new order, China has worked within existing institutions like the WTO, the UN, and regional forums, which is inconsistent with the behavior of an expansionist state.

China’s actions in the South China Sea

China’s activities in the South China Sea are an important topic of discussion during this debate.  Among the actions that must be analyzed are: the building of artificial islands, deployment of patrol ships, and the Nine-Dash Line. All these activities seem to clearly reflect an expansionist strategy from China’s end. Particularly, the transformation of reefs into military points has raised alarm in the international sphere and increased U.S. accusations of territorial aggression. However, a deeper analysis of China’s motivations suggests a more complex answer, one that aligns more with a defensive strategy than expansionism.

China’s actions in the South China Sea must be understood in the context of historical insecurity and maritime vulnerability. The region is a vital path for Chinese trade and energy imports. In fact, over 80% of China’s oil imports pass through these waters.[14] From China’s perspective, securing access to these sea lanes is fundamental for their survival. China’s recent growth has been strong primarily because of its economic development. The protection of these maritime routes is vital if China wants to continue thriving and protecting its economy. China has historically seen international maritime interventions take place, and is now under alert considering the United States’ aggressive posturing towards them.  As Nien-Chung Chang Liao explains, China’s assertiveness is “reactive rather than proactive,” shaped by domestic concerns and a sense of encirclement by western countries.[15]

Those who argue in favor of China having expansionist ambitions, often cite the The Nine-Dash Line as evidence. The line itself was not invented by the Chinese Communist Party but first appeared in a 1947 map issued by the Republic of China.[16] Though the line lacks recognition under international law, China views it as part of a historical narrative tied to national pride and past humiliation. The line is highly controversial because it overlaps with the exclusive economic zones (EEZs) of several Southeast Asian countries, including Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei.[17] It is important to prioritize China’s intentions when discussing this topic. China’s goal is defensive control of its surroundings to reduce vulnerability and intervention. Part of achieving this includes protecting its surrounding waters. On the other hand, expansionist states taking on these types of actions aim to gain new territory or overthrow other regimes, which is far from what China seeks to do in the South China Sea.  

Regarding the Nine Dash Line, China’s reliance on non-military instruments is very different to the behavior of traditional expansionist powers such as the United States. Expansionist states have historically used military force to invade, occupy, or impose regime change (such as U.S. interventions in Iraq in 2003 or Panama in 1989).[18] China has instead chosen low-intensity tactics designed to avoid open conflict. In the South China Sea, China does not rely primarily on its navy to assert dominance. Rather, it uses civilian and paramilitary actors such as the maritime militia and coast guard to enforce its claims.[19] This indirect approach allows China to demonstrate control while reducing the likelihood of military confrontation. These are actions not commonly taken by expansionist powers.

As Michael Studeman explains, Chinese leaders employ these tools “to signal resolve without incurring unacceptable risk.”[20] If China were acting with expansionist intent, it would likely adopt more aggressive military measures, seizing disputed territory by force. Yet China avoids escalation, uses limited pressure, and remains cautious in its engagement. This pattern suggests a calculated strategy based on insecurity and for regime preservation, not hegemonic ambition.

Evidently China’s behavior in the South China Sea has not followed the pattern of traditional expansionist powers. It has not invaded or annexed other countries, nor has it attempted regime change. Instead, it relies on a combination of patrols, diplomatic resistance, and controlled escalation. It seeks to protect the region’s waters by securing trade routes; there are no expansionist or territorial intentions. The South China Sea is an area vulnerable to intervention and China has taken on the role of protecting it, seeking regional stability, not more power.

Road and Belt Initiative

Those who argue China is taking on an expansionist strategy interpret the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) as evidence that it seeks to expand its geopolitical influence. The BRI is said to extend China’s economic reach while creating political leverage over weaker states with infrastructure projects stretching across Asia, Africa, Europe, and Latin America. A relevant example often cited is Sri Lanka’s Hambantota Port, which was leased to a Chinese state-owned company for 99 years after Sri Lanka was unable to meet its debt obligations. Critics argue that this is a case of “debt-trap diplomacy,”[21] where China extends unsustainable loans and seizes strategic assets when partners default—turning economic influence into control. These analysts claim that by funding major infrastructure while tying countries into long-term obligations, China is slowly building new spheres of influence. They argue that China’s long-term goal is to undermine U.S. dominance and position itself as the center of a new global order.

However, when analyzed through the logic of realism in international relations, this critique can be easily refuted. Realism suggests that in a system without an overarching authority, states cannot rely on goodwill or morality. China’s leaders, like all realists, assume that others will pursue their own interests, so they must do the same, the BRI is an example of this. The BRI allows China to diversify trade routes, secure access to resources, and stabilize the region, not dominate it. It addresses core insecurities: dependence on sea routes controlled by the U.S., underdevelopment in China’s western regions, and fear of encirclement by U.S. allies.

Moreover, the BRI has shown to not only benefit China's interests, but also deliver significant improvements to many developing countries. According to World Bank data, BRI-related infrastructure could boost global GDP by 0.7% by 2030, and lift 32 million people out of moderate poverty by improving connectivity and reducing trade costs.[22] A 2023 Nature study confirms that participation in the BRI has led to long-term poverty reduction, particularly in lower-middle-income countries, by enhancing infrastructure and access to finance. Southeast Asian countries such as Indonesia and Laos have gained new high-speed railways and power plants, while African countries like Kenya and Ethiopia have received critical transport and energy investments.[23]

Survey results from AidData’s “BRI From the Ground Up” also supports this point of view. According to the report, countries that joined the BRI saw an average 63% increase in official Chinese financing, compared to a 47% increase in non-BRI countries.[24] This indicates that BRI membership unlocks significant development assistance rather than political manipulation. Also, the loans China gives through the BRI have become more generous over time. For example, the share of loans that don’t need to be fully repaid (called the “grant element”) went up from 32.5% in 2013 to 35% in 2017.[25] This shows that China is trying to make its funding more helpful, not trap countries in debt. On top of that, leaders in BRI countries give China much higher approval. Results show about 20 percentage points more than leaders in non-BRI countries.[26] This suggests that these governments see China as a trusted economic partner, not a country trying to control them. Together, this data challenges the idea that the BRI is about domination. Instead, it supports the view that China’s main goal is to build economic partnerships and keep the region stable.

Economic partnerships: China vs. U.S.A

The way China manages economic partnerships supports the argument that they do not have expansionist or aggressive intentions. Rather, they act for economic reasons, looking out for their interest and protecting themselves from the international system.  In the 2025 Morning Consult survey reported by Axios, people in 41 countries were asked which global power they preferred to work with economically: China or the United States.  In the end, China received a net favorability score of +8.8, while the U.S. scored –1.5.[27] This means more people around the world had a positive view of China than of the United States, a big change compared to past years. This was especially true for countries in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, as well as those participating in the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). One major reason for this preference is that China often provides quick financing with fewer political or bureaucratic conditions than Western institutions like the IMF or World Bank. These funds go directly to large infrastructure projects like railways, ports, and energy systems, which are often difficult to fund through Western sources. Countries like Kenya, Ethiopia, and Pakistan have benefited from such projects, which were previously overlooked by Western donors.

        This survey further supports the argument that China’s intentions when enforcing their foreign policy is far from expansionist. If they were, their actions would look similar to those of the United States. China would tie economic aid with political influence, exporting ideology and intervening in domestic affairs. Instead, China’s strategy relies on offering economic alternatives, especially to countries overlooked by the Western financial system. Its focus on infrastructure and trade, without imposing ideological alignment, shows a preference for influence through development rather than domination.

Reaction to Anti-china ideology and global instability

Another important reason China’s behavior aligns with a defensive realist strategy is the increasingly hostile international environment shaped by the rise of populism, nationalism, and anti-China sentiment. In recent years, many Western democracies have seen the growth of far-right political movements that promote protectionism, anti-immigration laws, and distrust of foreign influence, especially from China. In the United States, President Donald Trump’s administration branded China a “strategic rival”. They imposed sweeping tariffs, and repeatedly blamed it for global crises. Similar patterns have emerged in Europe, where countries like Hungary, France, and Italy have seen far-right parties grow stronger while calling for tighter borders, investment controls, and even bans on Huawei or Chinese educational exchanges. In Australia and Canada, anti-Asian racism increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, further intensifying public hostility toward China.[28]

In this kind of global environment, it is risky and irrational for China to act like an expansionist power. Instead, China has responded by adopting a strategy of self-protection and risk management. Defensive realism explains this kind of behavior. When the international system becomes more unstable and threatening, states act to preserve their sovereignty, protect their economy, and avoid direct conflict.[29] China’s behavior reflects exactly that. For instance, the Belt and Road Initiative, as previously mentioned, is designed not to take over foreign governments but to secure long-term access to markets and resources. This is especially true as global trade becomes more politicized.

Even China’s diplomatic messaging reflects defensiveness. As said by Xi Jinping in 2022, “China will never seek hegemony or engage in expansionism. China stands firmly against all forms of hegemonism and power politics…”[30] Even as the international order destabilizes, China continues to avoid building military alliances or foreign bases on the scale of the U.S or other big powers. This is the behavior of a country trying to defend itself in a world growing more unpredictable and hostile, not of one trying to change the global order. As the international system becomes more fragmented, China’s best option is not expansion but stability and survival. As Susan Shirk explains, “China’s leaders are haunted by the fear of losing control” both internally and internationally. This makes regime security their top priority, not regional dominance.[31]

Conclusion:

In conclusion, the evidence presented in this paper strongly supports the argument that China under Xi Jinping is not pursuing an expansionist strategy, but rather acting defensively to protect its sovereignty and ensure regime survival. While some scholars interpret China’s assertiveness as signs of revisionist ambition, a closer analysis supported by defensive realism theories reveals that China’s behavior is shaped primarily by insecurity, not conquest.

This insecurity is rooted not only in historical trauma from the “Century of Humiliation,” but also in a deep-seated fear of exclusion, encirclement, and discrimination in the international system. China has long felt marginalized by a Western-led order that it did not design, and this feeling has intensified in recent years. The rise of anti-China sentiment in Western democracies, discriminatory narratives during the COVID-19 pandemic, and growing protectionism have reinforced a national perception that China is actively targeted. From Huawei bans to the labeling of China as a “strategic rival,” Beijing perceives an attempt to contain its rise and isolate it diplomatically, technologically, and economically.

These perceptions shape China’s foreign policy, including the BRI, its naval development, and its strategic partnerships. These actions can be understood as efforts to reduce dependence on hostile powers, secure critical resources, and ensure it is never again vulnerable to foreign coercion. Even its assertive strategy, often perceived as aggressive, reflects a desire to resist what it views as systemic bias and encirclement.

In this light, China’s rise should not be seen as a threat to international order, but a strategic adjustment of a state seeking to protect itself in a world it perceives as adversarial. The international community must recognize that China’s intentions are not to dominate others, but to avoid being dominated again. Ultimately, China seems less interested in rewriting global rules than in making sure it still has a seat at the table.
