Gender and Politics in the Arab World

Who Speaks for Arab Women? Gender, Power and Representation

Emilia Pintado Mariscal April 2026 7 min read

Few figures have been as politically useful as the “oppressed Muslim woman.” Her image has been invoked to justify colonial rule, military intervention and laws on what women may wear, and it still shapes how much of the world sees the Arab region. These representations have been shaped by colonial discourse, political agendas and competing feminist ideas. This essay examines how gender and power interact in the Arab world through four debates: Orientalism, gender as a category of analysis, colonial feminism and intersectionality. It argues that gender must be situated within the systems of power that produce it, and that this matters today, as representations of Muslim women continue to influence political discourse, policy decisions and global perceptions.

Understanding gender in this context requires examining how knowledge about the region has been historically produced. For this, Edward Said’s Orientalism text becomes useful. Representations of Muslim women, especially the recurring image of oppression, actively shape how reality is understood. These representations have often been used to construct a contrast between a “progressive” West and a supposedly “backward” East, making gender a central marker of difference. This dynamic remains visible in contemporary political discourse, for example in the justification of the War in Afghanistan, where the liberation of women was invoked to legitimize intervention. As Said argues, “the Orient is not an inert fact of nature,” but rather “a European invention.”[1] In this sense, gender becomes part of the process through which knowledge is produced and sustained.

Joan Scott’s work helps further develop this analysis by explaining why debates about women’s roles often carry broader political meaning. She argues that gender is not just a social category, but “a primary way of signifying relationships of power.”[2] This means that gender shapes how differences between groups are defined and understood. From this perspective, issues such as veiling, education, or legal reform are not only about individual choices, but about wider questions of identity and authority. This can be seen in policies that regulate how Muslim women dress, for example, France in the early 2000s.[3] In these cases, gender is used to reflect broader social and political concerns. The veil is not treated only as a personal or religious choice, but as something with political meaning. This supports Scott’s argument that gender, on top of showing inequality, also helps create and maintain it.

Understanding gender in this way also shows that the language of women’s rights is not neutral. Talking about equality does not always mean people are being treated equally. Sometimes it is used to justify or hide unequal power. Historically, this is evident in colonial contexts, where the “saving” of women was framed as part of a civilizing mission. This dynamic is analyzed by Leila Ahmed, who shows that “the idea of Islam as oppressive to women” became central to narratives of Western superiority.[4] Rather than simply promoting women’s rights, these discourses often reinforced broader hierarchies between the West and the Arab World.

A similar dynamic can be observed in more recent contexts. For example, reforms in Saudi Arabia, such as allowing women to drive, are often presented as evidence of progress. However, these changes are introduced and controlled by the state, which decides when and how rights are expanded. At the same time, some women who have publicly advocated for these rights have faced restrictions or punishment.[5] This shows that while women’s rights may increase in certain areas, the overall distribution of power does not necessarily change. Instead, these reforms coexist with systems that continue to limit women’s political and social influence. In this sense, gender remains closely tied to power, not only through restriction, but also through the way change itself is managed.[6]

This also shows the limits of trying to explain women’s experiences through a single, universal framework. When gender is shaped by different forms of power, it cannot be understood in the same way across all contexts. Representations that portray Muslim women mainly as victims in need of rescue rely on a narrow view of oppression and ignore the diversity of their experiences. In places such as Afghanistan, these narratives often reduce complex social realities to simplified images, overlooking how women act and make decisions within constraints. This critique is developed by Lila Abu-Lughod, who emphasizes the importance of analyzing women’s lives within their specific social and historical contexts, rather than applying external or universal categories.[7]

Looking at these issues more closely also requires considering how different forms of inequality overlap. As Kimberlé Crenshaw argues, “the intersectional experience is greater than the sum of racism and sexism.”[8] This means that gender cannot be separated from factors such as race, class, or nationality. When these dimensions are taken into account, it becomes clear that women’s experiences cannot be reduced to a single narrative of oppression, but must be understood in relation to multiple and interconnected structures of power.

This focus on context also highlights the importance of recognizing feminist thought within the region itself. When women’s experiences are interpreted mainly through external eyes, the long history of intellectual and political engagement by women in the Arab world is often overlooked. Opening the Gates by Margot Badran and Miriam Cooke, challenges this assumption by bringing together a century of Arab feminist writing, showing that debates about women’s roles, rights, and identities have long been part of local intellectual traditions.[9] Rather than presenting feminism as a recent or imported concept, the text demonstrates how these ideas have developed in relation to colonialism, nationalism, and social reform.

This perspective makes it possible to reinterpret contemporary moments more critically. For example, during the Arab Spring, women in countries like Egypt and Tunisia were highly visible in protests and political mobilization. However, their participation was not reflected in political outcomes. In Egypt, women held less than 2% of seats in the first post-revolution parliament.[10] This shows that while women played a key role in mobilization, existing power structures remained largely unchanged.[11]

Taken together, these readings show that gender in the Arab world cannot be understood as a simple or fixed issue, but must be analyzed in relation to how knowledge and power operate. Women’s experiences are shaped by historical contexts, political interests, and unequal global relationships, which influence how they are represented and interpreted. At the same time, these works highlight the importance of local perspectives, where women’s roles and feminist thought have developed within specific social and political conditions.

This approach challenges narratives that portray Arab and Muslim women as passive victims or symbols of cultural difference. It points to the need for more careful, context-based analysis that takes into account how gender intersects with other forms of power. This perspective is especially important in contemporary debates where gender is often used to justify policies, interventions, or cultural judgments. It allows for a more accurate understanding of both inequality and change, while recognizing the complexity of life experiences.
