Fragmented Foundations: The Enduring Instability of the Middle East’s State System

Emilia Pintado Mariscal June 2024 11 min read

Following World War 1, the Arab world entered the international relations arena at the Versailles Peace Conference. (Fawcett, 2018). Consequently, a State system was established in the Middle East, which further developed during the inter-war period. It can be said that from its foundation, this system has exhibited characteristics of inherent instability.  This essay explores the complex dynamics that have contributed to this. These factors include the impacts of Arab identity, arbitrary state boundaries, authoritarian governments and foreign mandates. It is important to state that it is not equivalent to discuss regional stability and a stable state system. Hence, the essay will further delve into the dilemma that both authors Fred Halliday and Louise Fawcett point to, where the Middle East region exhibits significant instability, yet the state system itself remains paradoxically stable.

First, we must define what makes a state system stable or unstable. Stability in a state system refers to the ability of state structures to maintain political continuity, manage diversity, and ensure socio-economic development. In contrast, instability manifests as a chronic inability of a state to provide these elements, characterized by frequent internal conflicts, economic disparities, and ineffective governance. (United States Institute of Peace)

To comprehend how the Middle Eastern system functions, we must go back to its history.  During the Ottoman Empire, ties with Europe were strong, however, they were neither just nor equal. European consuls were located in all the major cities in the Middle East, while local Arab Muslims had nearly no contact with European diplomats and no experience with International Relations (Fawcett, 2018). Looking back, the Middle East began its international journey at a disadvantage, leading to the inherent instability of the region.

During that time, the governance of the international system was in the hands of the European powers, which presented a problem for the Middle Eastern countries as they joined the international arena. Admittance to international society was conditional on recognition of sovereignty (Fawcett, 2018), and “states do not have sovereignty apart from recognition of it by others” (Bull, 1984). International recognition involves the acknowledgment by other states and international organizations of a political entity's sovereignty over a territory. Once a state is recognized, it gains certain rights, such as the ability to enter into treaties, membership in international organizations, and the right to diplomatic exchanges. At the time, many of the Middle Eastern countries were not recognized internationally, leaving uncertainty as to their sovereignty.

Once they were recognized by European powers, the Arabs prepared to join the international society and were intervened with another problem: those who had formerly been part of the Ottoman Empire had never learned to function in international relations because they had always been conducted by the Empire. (Fawcett, 2018). This caused another layer of instability in the Middle East. Governments were weak and incapable of handling foreign policy and external relations.

As European powers assumed control over the  Middle East, one of the treaties that took place was the Sykes-Picot Agreement. It was a secret treaty signed in 1916 between Great Britain and France. Its primary purpose was to divide the Ottoman Empire’s Arab provinces between the Allied powers, defining spheres of influence and control in the anticipation of an Ottoman defeat. France received direct control over what is now northern Syria and Lebanon, while Britain took control over the areas around the southern part of Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq) and modern-day Israel and Palestine. France had a sphere of influence over parts of Turkey, Iraq, and Syria. Britain received a similar sphere over what is now Jordan, southern Iraq, and other small territories (Fawcett, 2018).

Further on, as expected, the collapse of the Ottoman Empire after World War I created a power vacuum that was swiftly filled by European powers. During this time, Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, established a mandate system that placed large parts of the Middle East under British and French control. They said their objective was to prepare them for self-governance, but effectively they were treated as colonies. The Mandates System was criticized for being a veiled form of colonialism. This system undermined the legitimacy of the newly formed state structures and fueled nationalist movements that led to repeated uprisings. (Ibidem)

Next, The Treaty of Sèvres, signed in 1920, aimed to dismantle the Ottoman Empire after World War I by redistributing its lands to the Allied powers. It proposed new nations and changed borders without considering the ethnic and cultural realities of the region. However, strong resistance from Turkish nationalists led by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk prevented many of its terms from being implemented. The inability to implement these provisions speaks to the difficulties of applying Western-designed solutions to the complex regional dynamics of the Middle East, worsening ethnic tensions and contributing to ongoing regional conflicts. (Ibidem)

“Distinct peoples and cultures have existed for centuries, but nations as political entities are products of modernity, that is to put a date on it, since 1780” (Halliday, 2005). By ignoring the historical, cultural, and social characteristics of the region, the newly imposed state boundaries often forced disparate groups to live within the confines of a single political entity. This grouping could include people of different religions, ethnicities, and linguistic backgrounds. The search for identity in a state with so many different backgrounds is what led to the rise of nationalism in the regions. People were looking to feel represented within a state that was imposed on them. “This prevalence of nationalism as a principle of identity and statehood was not only a cause of inter-state conflict but was also often disputed within each state in terms of definition and precise goals” (Halliday, 2005). This means that even though a state may not be in tension with another external actor, its internal nationalism may be in conflict.

What is intriguing about the Middle East region is that regardless of this instability, the concept of the state remained intact even in areas experiencing significant internal discord. This resilience of the state concept means that despite internal fragmentation and conflicts, the state continues to exist as a recognized player on the international stage, maintaining its external dealings and obligations.

“How the Middle East was shaped in the inter-war years produced several stable states that have, with time, established themselves in the community of nations. It also gave rise to enduring problems that have troubled the region and the international order” (Fawcett, 2018). An example of conflicting territories is the British mandate of Palestine. This problem dates back to the Balfour Declaration, where false promises were made to both the Palestinians and the Jewish community. It was to be expected that the region would be inherently unstable given that its roots are so conflicting. This problem led to the uprising of Palestinian nationalist protests reclaiming their land. Throughout the years, the conflict has escalated and become more and more complex. Now, it is close to impossible to find a fair solution to this, which is why Palestine and Israel have been in constant conflict since the 1920’s until today.

Another factor that significantly complicated the political landscape during the formation of the Middle East was the lack of consensus among Arab leaders and populations about the desired structure of their state system. This disagreement stemmed from distinct visions for the region's future. On one side, some Arab nationalists, inspired by the pan-Arabism promoted by figures like Sharif Hussein of Mecca, advocated for the creation of a single, large Arab state that would encompass most of the Arab-speaking regions of the former Ottoman Empire. This vision was fueled by a sense of shared cultural and linguistic heritage and was seen as a way to unite the Arab world against external influences and control.

On the other hand, other groups and leaders pushed for the establishment of individual national states, each with its own sovereignty and defined borders. Proponents of this approach argued that smaller, independent states would be more manageable and more directly reflect the diverse interests and identities of their populations.

This fundamental disagreement led to a fragmented approach to nation-building in the region. Without a clear, unified strategy, the newly formed states struggled to establish stable governance structures. As I mentioned in multiple previous arguments, given that the foundation of the Middle East was unstable, it was inevitable that it would be conflicting throughout its development as a region. The Middle East emerged from the post-World War I period with a patchwork of states that often had poorly defined national identities and conflicted loyalties, laying the groundwork for a century of political instability.

The fact that the Middle East was unstable since it became a region, led its countries to adopt certain politics in an attempt to solve the issue. Many of the countries have adopted authoritarian regimes as a means of maintaining control over their diverse and often divided populations. These regimes typically suppress political dissent, relying on repressive measures. The lack of strong political institutions means that the government often relies on coercion rather than consensus. This authoritarian approach has worsened rather than resolved conflicts, creating a facade of stability that masks deeper issues.

While authoritarian measures can create an image of order, they fail to address the profound causes of conflict. Therefore, the stability of these regimes is superficial and can be easily disrupted. The long-term consequences of authoritarian governance do not favor the social and political aspects of Middle Eastern states. The suppression of political freedoms, the lack of transparency, and the large economic inequalities limit the development of functioning societies. Over time, these factors affect the legitimacy of the state and its institutions, making it increasingly difficult to govern effectively.

Moreover, the reliance on repression to maintain control means that any significant challenge to the regime, whether from economic crises, social movements, or external pressures, can lead to a rapid and violent unraveling of the state. This is why the Middle East is such a violent region overall. Also, once the people rise against the authoritarian regime, arrive periods of intense instability.

When talking about the Middle East, there is a complex and intriguing aspect of the region's political and historical analysis. There is a paradox regarding regional stability versus state system stability in the Middle East. It highlights a significant contradiction where the broader region shows signs of chronic instability, yet the state systems within, in terms of their borders and governmental structures, appear remarkably stable.

Despite internal conflicts, the international community generally upholds the principle of state sovereignty and territorial integrity as per international law and norms established by the United Nations. Therefore, even unstable regimes often receive recognition which reinforces the stability of the state system regardless of internal conflicts.

The state systems in the Middle East were designed through agreements and mandates that paid little attention to the ethnic, tribal, or religious continuities in the region. Despite frequent tensions, coups, and uprisings within these states, the region has mostly upheld the territorial integrity of these borders. The principle of state sovereignty has reinforced these boundaries, even when the states themselves are involved in deep internal conflicts.

It can be argued that in the Middle East, external powers have played a significant role in maintaining the stability of state systems. For example, throughout the Cold War and beyond, both the United States and the Soviet Union, have provided military, economic, and diplomatic support to various regimes in the region. It is difficult to say if the region could have maintained a stable state system on its own.

Throughout this essay, the goal was to answer the following statement: ‘The state system that emerged in the Middle East at the end of the inter-war period was inherently unstable.’ After having analyzed all of these factors, it is fair to conclude that to respond to this argument, it is necessary to divide it into two parts. First, the stability within each state and the relations of the states with each other, and on the other hand, the stability of the system, referring to the movement of borders and recognition of States.

Having understood this, it is fair to conclude that within the states, the Middle East emerged as an inherently unstable region.The imposition of arbitrary state boundaries by colonial powers ignored the complex ethnic, and religious characteristics of the region. Also, the mandate system further complicated the situation by placing large parts of the Middle East under British and French control. This approach undermined the legitimacy of newly formed state structures and fueled nationalist movements that frequently erupted into violence. Finally, the lack of strong political institutions capable of managing societal conflicts meant that governance often depended on repression rather than consensus, creating a facade of stability that masked deeper issues. The lack of political representation, and social injustice, led to fragile state structures that could be easily disrupted.

However, despite these internal challenges, the principle of state sovereignty and territorial integrity has maintained the external borders of Middle Eastern states. The international community, driven by personal interests, has often supported authoritarian regimes, reinforcing the stability of state systems.

In conclusion, the inherent instability of the Middle Eastern state system established at the end of the inter-war period can be attributed to a combination of historical, political, and economic factors. However, eventhough there are sources of constant conflict in the region, the phenomenon of stability of the state system as a whole must not be ignored.
